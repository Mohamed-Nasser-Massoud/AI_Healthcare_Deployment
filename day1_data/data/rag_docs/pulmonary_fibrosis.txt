Pulmonary fibrosis is a condition in which lung tissue becomes scarred and thickened over time, making it increasingly difficult for oxygen to pass from the lungs into the bloodstream. The scarring is generally irreversible, and as it progresses, the lungs become stiffer and less able to expand fully, leading to progressively worsening shortness of breath. In many cases, no specific cause can be identified, a form known as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), which tends to affect older adults and progresses at a variable but generally steady pace. Known causes and contributing factors in other cases include long-term exposure to certain dusts or chemicals (such as asbestos or silica), certain autoimmune diseases (like rheumatoid arthritis or scleroderma), some medications, radiation therapy to the chest, and chronic conditions like gastroesophageal reflux disease. Symptoms typically develop gradually and include shortness of breath — especially during physical activity — a persistent dry cough, fatigue, unintentional weight loss, and widening/rounding of the fingertips and nails (a sign called clubbing) in more advanced cases. Diagnosis usually involves a combination of a high-resolution CT scan of the chest, pulmonary function tests to measure lung capacity, and sometimes a lung biopsy to characterize the scarring pattern. While there's no cure that reverses existing scarring, treatments including antifibrotic medications can slow disease progression, and pulmonary rehabilitation, supplemental oxygen, and in eligible patients, lung transplantation, can help manage symptoms and improve quality of life.
