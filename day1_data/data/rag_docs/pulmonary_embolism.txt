A pulmonary embolism (PE) is a sudden blockage in one of the pulmonary arteries in the lungs, usually caused by a blood clot that has traveled from elsewhere in the body — most commonly the deep veins of the legs, a condition called deep vein thrombosis (DVT). When a clot breaks loose and travels through the bloodstream to the lungs, it can partially or completely block blood flow, which is a medical emergency. Risk factors include prolonged immobility (such as long flights or bed rest after surgery), certain surgeries, cancer, pregnancy, hormone therapy or birth control pills, smoking, obesity, and inherited clotting disorders. Symptoms can develop suddenly and include shortness of breath (often the most prominent symptom), sharp chest pain that worsens with deep breathing or coughing, rapid heart rate, coughing (sometimes with blood-tinged sputum), lightheadedness, or fainting. A large PE can be life-threatening very quickly because it prevents oxygen from reaching the bloodstream efficiently and puts sudden strain on the heart. Diagnosis often involves a CT pulmonary angiogram, blood tests (like a D-dimer test), and sometimes an ultrasound of the legs to check for DVT. Treatment typically centers on blood-thinning medication (anticoagulants) to prevent the clot from growing and allow the body to gradually dissolve it, and in severe cases, clot-dissolving drugs or procedures to physically remove the clot may be needed. Anyone experiencing sudden shortness of breath with chest pain should seek emergency care immediately, since prompt treatment significantly improves outcomes.
