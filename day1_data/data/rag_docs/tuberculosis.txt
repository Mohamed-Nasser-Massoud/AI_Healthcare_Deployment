Tuberculosis (TB) is a bacterial infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis that most commonly affects the lungs, though it can spread to other organs as well. It spreads through the air when a person with active TB disease coughs, speaks, or sings, releasing tiny droplets that others can inhale. Importantly, TB infection exists on a spectrum: in latent TB infection, the bacteria are present in the body but inactive, causing no symptoms and being non-contagious, while in active TB disease, the bacteria are actively multiplying and causing illness, and the person can spread it to others. Symptoms of active pulmonary TB typically include a persistent cough lasting three weeks or longer (sometimes with blood), chest pain, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, fever, night sweats, and loss of appetite. Diagnosis involves a combination of skin or blood tests to check for TB infection, chest X-rays to look for lung involvement, and sputum tests to confirm active disease and identify the specific bacterial strain. Treatment for active TB requires a lengthy course of multiple antibiotics, typically taken for six to nine months, and it's critical to complete the full course even after symptoms improve — stopping early is a major driver of drug-resistant TB strains, which are much harder to treat. TB remains a significant global health issue, particularly in regions with limited healthcare access, and is closely linked to factors like overcrowding, malnutrition, and weakened immune systems (including HIV co-infection).
