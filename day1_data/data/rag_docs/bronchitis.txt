Bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchial tubes, the airways that carry air to and from the lungs, and it comes in two main forms: acute and chronic. Acute bronchitis is usually caused by the same viruses responsible for colds and the flu, develops over a few days, and typically resolves within one to three weeks as the body clears the infection; antibiotics generally aren't helpful since the cause is viral, not bacterial. Chronic bronchitis, on the other hand, is a long-term condition defined by a persistent productive cough lasting at least three months a year for two consecutive years, and it's actually classified as one of the two main forms of COPD, most often caused by long-term smoking or exposure to lung irritants. Symptoms of acute bronchitis include a cough that may produce clear, yellow, or greenish mucus, chest discomfort, mild fever, fatigue, and sometimes wheezing or shortness of breath. Chronic bronchitis shares these symptoms but persists or recurs over months and years, and often worsens gradually. Management for acute bronchitis is mainly supportive: rest, fluids, and over-the-counter symptom relief, since it usually clears on its own. Chronic bronchitis management focuses on the same strategies used for COPD more broadly — smoking cessation being the most important step, along with bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, and pulmonary rehabilitation to manage symptoms and slow progression.
