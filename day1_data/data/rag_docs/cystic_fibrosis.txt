Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the CFTR gene, which affects the movement of salt and water in and out of cells. This leads to the production of unusually thick, sticky mucus that can clog the airways in the lungs and block ducts in other organs, particularly the pancreas. It's an inherited condition — a person needs to receive a mutated copy of the gene from both parents to have CF — and it's typically diagnosed in infancy or early childhood, often through newborn screening programs in many countries. In the lungs, the thick mucus makes it difficult to clear bacteria, leading to frequent and sometimes severe lung infections, chronic cough, wheezing, and progressive lung damage over time. Beyond the respiratory system, CF commonly affects digestion, since thick mucus can block the ducts that carry digestive enzymes from the pancreas to the intestines, leading to poor nutrient absorption, and it can also affect fertility. There is no cure for CF, but treatment has advanced substantially in recent decades, including airway clearance techniques to help loosen and clear mucus, inhaled medications to thin mucus and fight infection, pancreatic enzyme replacement to aid digestion, and — more recently — a class of drugs called CFTR modulators that target the underlying genetic defect itself in patients with specific mutations, significantly improving lung function and quality of life for many. With ongoing advances in care, life expectancy for people with CF has increased substantially over the past several decades.
