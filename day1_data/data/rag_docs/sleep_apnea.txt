Sleep apnea is a condition in which breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, and the most common form, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), occurs when the muscles in the back of the throat relax excessively and block the airway. A less common form, central sleep apnea, happens when the brain fails to send proper signals to the muscles that control breathing. Each pause in breathing, called an apnea, can last from a few seconds to over a minute and may happen dozens or even hundreds of times a night in more severe cases, though the person is often unaware it's happening. Common symptoms include loud snoring, gasping or choking sounds during sleep, waking up with a dry mouth or sore throat, morning headaches, excessive daytime sleepiness even after a full night's sleep, difficulty concentrating, and irritability. Risk factors include excess weight (particularly around the neck), a naturally narrow airway, enlarged tonsils, smoking, alcohol use, and family history. Left untreated, sleep apnea is associated with an increased risk of high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes, largely because the repeated drops in blood oxygen levels put ongoing strain on the cardiovascular system. Diagnosis is typically confirmed with a sleep study, either in a sleep lab or with a home testing device, which monitors breathing patterns, oxygen levels, and other measures overnight. Treatment options range from lifestyle changes (weight loss, avoiding alcohol before bed, sleeping on one's side) to CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure) machines, which are the most common and effective treatment for moderate to severe OSA, and in some cases oral appliances or surgery.
