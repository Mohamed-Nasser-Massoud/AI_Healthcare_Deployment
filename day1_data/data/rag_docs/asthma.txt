Asthma is a chronic condition in which the airways become inflamed, narrowed, and produce extra mucus, making breathing difficult. Unlike COPD, asthma's airflow limitation is typically reversible, either on its own or with medication, and it often begins in childhood, though it can develop at any age. Common triggers include allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander, mold), respiratory infections, cold air, exercise, air pollution, strong odors, and stress. During an asthma attack, the muscles around the airways tighten (bronchospasm) and the airway lining swells and produces mucus, all of which narrow the space air can pass through. Typical symptoms include wheezing (a whistling sound when breathing), shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing, which is often worse at night or early in the morning. Severity ranges widely: some people have mild, infrequent symptoms, while others experience frequent flare-ups that significantly affect daily life. Asthma is usually managed with two categories of medication: quick-relief (rescue) inhalers, typically short-acting bronchodilators used during an attack to rapidly open the airways, and long-term control medications, often inhaled corticosteroids, taken regularly to reduce underlying airway inflammation and prevent attacks from happening in the first place. An asthma action plan, developed with a healthcare provider, helps patients recognize worsening symptoms early and know when to adjust medication or seek emergency care. Severe, uncontrolled asthma attacks can be life-threatening and require immediate medical attention.
